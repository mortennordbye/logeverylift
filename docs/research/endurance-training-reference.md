> Source of truth for the training rules the AI coach and the endurance features use. Research done 2026-11-12 from peer-reviewed studies, consensus statements and coach sources. Each rule carries an evidence label: Study, Consensus, Coach convention or Inference. Written for full-distance Ironman; the half-distance differences are at the end.

# Coach an age-group Ironman by rules

A good full-distance plan for an athlete with a job is not a fixed template. It is a small set of rules applied each week to fresh data. The evidence and coaching practice agree on a frame. Use 20-30 weeks: a general base, a race-specific build of about 12 weeks, and a 2-3 week taper. Organise the load in 3-week blocks, with 10-14 hours a week for most busy age-groupers, about half of it on the bike. Keep roughly 65-80% of time below the first threshold. Protect 2-3 key sessions a week. Long rides stop near 5-6 h and long runs near 2-2.5 h. Two heavy lifting sessions a week (at least 80% 1RM) improve running economy and cycling efficiency, then one session a week keeps the gain. An AI coach adds most value in day-to-day adaptation, and the evidence gives it clear limits there. HRV-guided training gives only a small performance benefit, so HRV changes the next session, not the plan. Garmin's composite scores are not validated, and its auto-detected threshold pace reads about 12% slow. The best-supported injury rule is per session: no run more than 10% longer than the longest run of the last 30 days. Consistency beats any single session: athletes who complete more than 80% of planned weeks are 7 times more likely to reach their goal. So the coach's job is to keep the athlete healthy and training, not to push. The athlete must also trust each change. Products that earn that trust show a proposed change, give the reason, and let the athlete accept it. Each rule below carries one of four evidence labels. **Study** means a peer-reviewed trial, meta-analysis or large cohort. **Consensus** means a position stand or expert consensus. **Coach convention** means published coach or vendor practice. **Inference** means a rule derived in this report from the sources, not tested directly.

## A 20-30 week plan is three phases built from 3-week blocks

### Linear periodization is the default, and reverse periodization is ruled out

Coach practice uses a traditional linear model. Joe Friel's version runs **base 9-12 weeks, build 8-9 weeks, peak 1-2 weeks** ([220 Triathlon](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/long-distance/conquer-ironman-part-1)). He calls the build, from about 12 weeks out to 3 weeks out, the most influential part of preparation. His reason: what matters most is "how long and race-like your workouts are in the last 12 weeks" ([220 Triathlon](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/long-distance/ironman-training-how-to-maximise-the-build-phase)). His build/peak plans use three 3-week blocks of two quality weeks and one recovery week ([Coach Ray on Friel](https://coachray.nz/2021/10/18/joe-friels-bible-for-periodisation)). Mikael Eriksson puts race-specific work in the **last 4-8 weeks**, and his full-distance plans run 20, 22 and 26 weeks ([TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/training-plans/triathlon/ironman/tp-284728/advanced-full-distance-triathlon-22-week-plan-bike-power-run-pace)). 80/20 Endurance plans run 22 weeks ([FinalSurge](https://finalsurge.com/club/team8020/plan/69000)). Matt Dixon (Purple Patch) plans in 3-week cycles with rotating emphasis, so not every weekend needs a long ride and a long run. He prefers progression all year to one 16-week build ([Purple Patch](https://purplepatchfitness.com/freetrainingtips/ironman-triathlon-success); [Purple Patch](https://www.purplepatchfitness.com/freetrainingtips/race-plans-are-history)).

The research does not support a more exotic model. The only triathlon RCT of reverse periodization found that the traditional group improved running and the reverse group did not ([Clemente-Suárez & Ramos-Campo 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6696421/)). A 2022 systematic review found reverse periodization no better on any endurance outcome, with low-quality evidence ([Sports Medicine Open 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9023617/)). No trial of block periodization in Ironman exists. In a 16-week macrocycle from one coach, recreational Ironman athletes reached **peak load around week 8** ([Esteve-Lanao 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00298/full)).

| Weeks to race | Phase | What the coach does | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30-12 (8-14 wk; 6-8 wk in a 20-wk plan) | General base | Aerobic volume, technique, heavy strength 2x/wk, run durability | Coach convention |
| 12-3 | Specific build | Long, race-like sessions; race-pace blocks grow; bricks; gut training | Coach convention |
| 6-3 | Race simulation | 1-2 full simulation days; longest sessions end about 3 weeks out | Coach convention |
| 3-0 | Taper | Volume down 41-60%, intensity and frequency kept | Study |
| All phases | Block unit | 3-week blocks: 2 load + 1 lighter (2:1); 3:1 for robust younger athletes | Coach convention; the 2:1 vs 3:1 split is inference |

### Hours, sport split and recovery weeks scale with the athlete's life

Recreational finishers (mean 11:45) trained a **mean 12.9 h/wk over 16 weeks, with a peak week of 20.9 h** ([Esteve-Lanao 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00298/full)). In a second group, total training time correlated with faster race time (r = -0.69) ([Muñoz 2014](https://observatorio-cientifico.ua.es/documentos/601df87ac877fc2530d4a179?lang=en)). Purple Patch sets **10-14 h/wk** for busy Ironman athletes. It calls 16-24 h plans unsustainable for most people with jobs and families ([Purple Patch](https://purplepatchfitness.com/freetrainingtips/ironman-triathlon-success)). Published plans span 5-19 h/wk across 80/20 levels ([80/20 Level 3](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/training-plans/triathlon/ironman/tp-112108/80-20-triathlon-ironman-level-3-heart-rate-7-to-19-hours-per-week)). Many coaches put **at least 50% of training time on the bike** ([Triathlete](https://www.triathlete.com/training/triathletes-complete-guide-on-how-to-train-for-an-ironman/?scope=anon)). Brett Sutton says triathletes do not need to run daily, and that daily running can hurt women and older athletes ([Triathlete](https://www.triathlete.com/training/essential-run-advice-coach-brett-sutton/?scope=anon)).

No source gives a tested week-to-week volume increase or a tested recovery-week cut. The CTL ramp limits in the next section are the most concrete numbers available.

| If | Then | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Athlete has 8-12 h/wk | Finish-focused tier; long ride peak 4.5-5 h; a larger share of moderate/hard work is acceptable | Coach convention (Purple Patch); tier cut-offs are inference |
| Athlete has 12-16 h/wk | Typical competitive tier; standard template | Inference from Esteve-Lanao and plan ranges |
| Athlete has 16-20+ h/wk | Add the extra hours as low intensity, not more threshold work | Coach convention ([Trizone](https://www.trizone.com.au/my-training-numbers-to-824-at-the-2018-ironman-world-championship/)) |
| Planning peak week | Peak week about 1.5-1.6x the average week, about mid-plan to 3 weeks out | Inference from Esteve-Lanao |
| Splitting hours by sport | Bike about 50%, run about 25-30%, swim about 15-25% (more swim share at low hours) | Bike share is coach convention; run/swim shares are inference |
| Athlete is 40+ or recovers slowly (HRV slow to rebound after load weeks) | Use 2:1 blocks, at least 48 h between hard sessions in the same sport | Inference; the masters sources are weak |
| Life gets busy | Cut supporting sessions first; keep the 2-3 key sessions | Coach convention ([Purple Patch](https://purplepatchfitness.com/freetrainingtips/ironman-triathlon-success)) |

### Mostly easy, with more race-pace work as the race gets close

Recreational Ironman athletes train pyramidally. One group spent **67.5% of time below the aerobic threshold, 28.4% between thresholds and 4.3% above the anaerobic threshold** ([Esteve-Lanao 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00298/full)). In another group, time below the first threshold correlated strongly with faster finish times (r = -0.92). Much zone 2 cycling may link to a worse result ([Muñoz 2014](https://observatorio-cientifico.ua.es/documentos/601df87ac877fc2530d4a179?lang=en)). An individual-patient-data network meta-analysis found **no difference between polarized and pyramidal** in VO2max or time-trial results. It did find a subgroup signal: recreational athletes may respond better to pyramidal ([Rosenblat 2025](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/2095779)). Polarized training wins only on VO2peak, mainly in interventions shorter than 12 weeks ([Oliveira 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428)). The race is almost all moderate intensity: Dan Plews' Ironman run was 96% between thresholds. So he moves from polarized early in the season to pyramidal near the race ([Trizone](https://www.trizone.com.au/polarised-to-pyramidal-training-intensity-distributions-the-principle-of-specificity-is-key/)). Purple Patch disagrees for time-starved athletes. At 8-12 h/wk, mostly easy training "will not likely provide a significant enough stimulus" ([Purple Patch](https://purplepatchfitness.com/freetrainingtips/ironman-triathlon-success)).

| If | Then | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Default week | 65-80% of time below LT1/VT1, 15-30% between LT1 and LT2, 3-10% above LT2 | Study (observational) plus coach convention |
| Base phase | Lean polarized: more Z1, a little above-LT2 work | Coach convention (Plews) |
| Last 8-12 weeks | Lean pyramidal: grow race-pace (LT1-LT2) segments inside long sessions | Coach convention plus specificity argument |
| Athlete under about 10 h/wk | Allow a higher share of moderate/hard work; keep the long ride and long run easy to steady | Coach convention (Purple Patch) |
| Unplanned bike time between thresholds keeps adding up outside race-pace blocks | Flag it; move the excess back to Z1 | Inference from Muñoz 2014 |

### Key sessions have ceilings, not targets to beat

Coaches build the **long ride to 5-6 h** ([Roadman Cycling](https://roadmancycling.com/blog/ironman-bike-training-plan-16-weeks); [220 Triathlon](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/long-distance/ironman-training-how-to-maximise-the-build-phase)). Chrissie Wellington, coached by Sutton, never rode over 5 h or ran over 2:20 ([220 Triathlon](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/beginners/chrissie-wellington-s-top-ten-lessons-for-triathletes)). One UK coach says it is "rare for athletes I coach to run beyond 2.5 hours" because of recovery cost ([Coach Cox](https://www.coachcox.co.uk/?p=2709)). TrainingPeaks spreads **4-6 race-pace sessions over the last 12 weeks**. The longest race-pace pieces it uses are about 1.9 km swim, 113 km bike and 26 km run ([TrainingPeaks](https://trainingpeaks.com/blog/how-to-fit-race-pace-workouts-into-your-ironman-training)). Dixon puts a big race-simulation day in week 3 of a cycle to test fuel and fluids ([Purple Patch](https://purplepatchfitness.com/freetrainingtips/ironman-triathlon-success)). Friel builds the longest swim to 4,000 m three weeks out ([220 Triathlon](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/long-distance/ironman-training-how-to-maximise-the-build-phase)). Every number here is coach practice. No study has tested optimal long-session length for Ironman.

| Session | Rule | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Long ride | Peak 5-6 h (4.5-5 h for low-volume athletes); about weekly in the build | Coach convention |
| Long run | Peak 2-2.5 h; 3 h only for robust, experienced runners; every 1-2 weeks, not always weekly | Coach convention |
| Race-pace blocks | 4-6 sessions in the last 12 weeks, growing toward about 113 km bike / 26 km run at race effort | Coach convention |
| Brick | Short run off the bike most build weeks; one long brick (4-5 h bike + 45-90 min run) every 2-3 weeks | Coach convention (low-authority source) plus inference |
| Race simulation | 1-2 days at 3-6 weeks out, with race fueling and kit | Coach convention |
| Last big session | About 3 weeks out, then taper | Coach convention |
| Key sessions per week | 2-3, protected; everything else is supporting | Coach convention |

### The taper cuts volume, not intensity or frequency

The classic meta-analysis found the best taper lasts **2 weeks, with volume cut exponentially by 41-60% and intensity and frequency kept** ([Bosquet 2007](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/bosquet.htm)). A 2023 meta-analysis of 14 endurance studies found tapers improve time-trial results (SMD -0.45). The effect was larger after a pre-taper overload, and the authors suggest **21 days or more** with a progressive 41-60% cut ([Wang 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37163550/)). A triathlon review gives 8-14 days for running and cycling and about 21 days for swimming ([Rev Bras Ciênc Mov 2017](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4059196?lng=en)). Sources disagree on the run. 220 Triathlon cuts run volume first because running causes the most muscle damage ([220 Triathlon](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/master-the-taper)). Precision Hydration cuts the run less, by 20-40% ([Precision Hydration](https://www.precisionhydration.com/blogs/hydration_advice/how-to-taper-for-triathlon)).

| If | Then | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Entering taper | 14-21 days; shorter for low-volume athletes, longer after a big overload block | Study (mixed endurance sports) |
| Each taper week | Volume falls progressively to 40-60% below peak; session count about the same; keep short race-pace and above-threshold efforts | Study |
| Choosing which sport to cut most | Unresolved; default to a larger run cut for injury-prone runners, a smaller run cut for durable ones | Inference (sources conflict) |
| Illness 8-12 days before the race | Cut taper intensity further; lower race-day expectations | Study ([IOC 2022](https://www.casem-acmse.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/1066.full_.pdf)) plus inference |

## One threshold per sport anchors every zone and load number

### Store anchors, derive zones

Research uses three zones anchored at VT1/LT1 and VT2/LT2 ([Sperlich 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1258585/pdf)). Coaching uses Coggan power zones from FTP and Friel heart-rate and pace zones from LTHR and threshold pace ([TrainingPeaks, Friel](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/joe-friel-s-quick-guide-to-setting-zones/)). FTP, LTHR and threshold pace all approximate the upper threshold, so the models share one anchor. None of them anchors VT1 directly.

| Zone | Coggan bike %FTP | Friel run %LTHR | Friel bike %LTHR | Research 3-zone (inferred map) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | <55 | <85 | <81 | Z1 (below VT1) |
| 2 | 55-74 | 85-89 | 81-89 | Z1 (VT1 near top of this zone) |
| 3 | 75-89 | 90-94 | 90-93 | Z2 (VT1-VT2) |
| 4 | 90-104 | 95-99 | 94-99 | Z2 |
| 5 / 5a-c | 105-120 | 100 to >106 | 100 to >106 | Z3 (above VT2) |

The %FTP and %LTHR columns come from [TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/joe-friel-s-quick-guide-to-setting-zones/). The right-hand column is inference: no validated crosswalk exists. For swimming, Friel uses "T-time" (a 1,000 m time trial divided by 10, then sets as T plus or minus seconds per 100). Critical swim speed is the slope between 200 m and 400 m efforts, CSS = (400 - 200) / (T400 - T200). CSS correlates with lactate-threshold speed, but short test distances can overestimate it ([Unesp](https://repositorio.unesp.br/bitstream/11449/42460/1/WOS000308574000004.pdf); [PMC6359490](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6359490)).

### Field tests beat Garmin estimates for setting zones

Friel's run and bike threshold test is a solo 30-minute all-out effort. The mean HR of the last 20 minutes approximates LTHR ([TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/joe-friel-s-quick-guide-to-setting-zones/)). VT1 can be found without a lab. DFA a1 = 0.75 from a chest strap matched gas-exchange VT1 closely (ICC 0.96 for HR) ([Rogers 2021](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-wuerzburg.de/files/22260/fphys-11-596567.pdf)), including with consumer straps ([J Sports Sci 2023](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2023.2277034)). Garmin's estimates are weaker. On the Fenix 7, auto-detected **pace at LT was 11.96% slower** than a blood-lactate test, while **HR at LT was only 1.71% low** ([Heiber 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11090120/)). Garmin VO2max error is about 3-7% on average, with larger error and underestimation in highly trained athletes ([PubMed 39797066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39797066/); [the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2025/08/27/garmin-vo2max-scientific-study-roasts-it-and-loves-it-when-a-it-dpends/)).

| If | Then | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Only Garmin auto LT pace exists | Do not set run pace zones from it (they will be too easy); use Garmin LTHR, or schedule a 30-min test | Study (small) |
| Need VT1 (top of easy) | Use DFA a1 = 0.75 from a chest strap, or estimate at about 70-75% FTP / about 89% run LTHR until tested | Study for DFA a1; the percentages are inference |
| Garmin VO2max moves | Treat it as a trend, never as a zone input | Study |
| Recovery week ends, or the athlete returns from illness, or session data shows a clear fitness change | Retest (every 4-8 weeks is a common convention; no tested interval exists) | Inference |
| Swim anchor | CSS from 400/200 m, or Friel T-time from 1,000 m | Coach convention plus study |

### Race intensity sets the ceiling for training load

TSS = hours x IF² x 100, with IF = NP / FTP ([Prommer](https://prommer.net/en/training/calculators/triathlon/ironman-tss-planner/)). TrainingPeaks puts a typical full-distance bike at **250-300 TSS** and the run at **190-250 TSS**. It warns not to repeat race-day run TSS in training ([TrainingPeaks](https://trainingpeaks.com/blog/how-to-use-tss-to-prepare-for-an-ironman)). Slower riders must ride a lower IF to stay inside that band ([TrainingPeaks](https://trainingpeaks.com/blog/how-to-cheat-by-using-a-power-meter-in-an-ironman)). First-timers are often told IF 0.65-0.70 ([Roadman Cycling](https://roadmancycling.com/blog/what-wattage-should-you-ride-in-an-ironman)). Median IF for 50+ TrainerRoad riders at Kona 2024 was 0.695 ([TrainerRoad](https://www.trainerroad.com/blog/the-kona-bike-course-how-hard-is-it-and-how-hard-do-athletes-actually-ride-it/)). Friel's durability test: decoupling of no more than **5% over 4 h of riding at goal effort and 2 h of race-like running** ([220 Triathlon](https://220triathlon.com/?p=8397)). In TrainingPeaks terms, under 5% shows good aerobic endurance, 5-10% shows limits or fatigue, and over 10% suggests the effort is above the aerobic threshold ([TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)).

| If | Then | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Setting bike race target | IF_target = sqrt(TSS_cap / (100 x expected bike hours)), TSS_cap 250-300. Example: 6 h gives IF about 0.68; 7 h gives about 0.63 | Coach convention; the formula rearrangement is inference |
| Long steady ride or run decouples under 5% at goal effort | Durability is adequate at that intensity; progress duration or race-pace share | Coach convention |
| Decoupling 5-10% | Hold progression; check heat, fueling and fatigue first | Coach convention |
| Decoupling over 10% | Planned intensity is likely above LT1; lower the target or re-check VT1 | Coach convention |
| Computing decoupling | Split the steady part (no warm-up) in half; EF = NP (or speed) / avg HR; decoupling % = (EF1 - EF2) / EF1 x 100; ignore hot or hilly sessions | Inference from the TrainingPeaks method |

### CTL ramp limits are conventions, and the ACWR is not an injury predictor

CTL and ATL are exponentially weighted averages of daily TSS with 42-day and 7-day time constants, and TSB = CTL - ATL ([Couzens](https://www.alancouzens.com/blog/CTLramp.html)). Friel calls **5-8 CTL/week** right for most athletes. Above 8 is the "crash training load" range, and above 10 suits only some elites for a week or less ([TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/why-ramp-rate-is-an-important-training-metric/)). TrainingPeaks rates a ramp of 3 or less per week as low risk for athletes at CTL 45 or below, 4-6 as moderate and 7 or more as high ([TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/understanding-trainingpeaks-ramp-rate-for-better-coaching/)). Alan Couzens suggests **3-5 CTL/week**, about +10 per month. He loads at CTL + 30 TSS/day and lets TSB reach about -20 at the end of three loading weeks. A 7-day recovery week at CTL - 30 TSS/day returns TSB to about 0. He puts top age-group CTL at about 150 ([Couzens](https://www.alancouzens.com/blog/CTLramp.html)).

The acute:chronic ratio does not hold up. Impellizzeri and colleagues showed that dividing acute load by chronic load only rescales the variable. Random "chronic" denominators gave similar associations, and neither the ratio nor acute load predicted injury meaningfully ([Impellizzeri 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938)). Garmin's Load Ratio (0.8-1.4 "optimal") is an ACWR built on EPOC, not TSS ([Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals-apac/webhelp/forerunner970/EN-SG/GUID-D26A0A2F-A150-4F77-9BDE-3686E5AB74C8-7193.html)). No validated conversion to CTL exists. No sourced race-day TSB target was found, so treat TSB as an output of the volume-based taper, not a goal.

| If | Then | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Planned 7-day CTL ramp over 8 | Reject or cut the week | Coach convention |
| CTL 45 or below and ramp over 6 | Cut to 3-5/week | Vendor/coach convention |
| Long-term planning | Target 3-5 CTL/week (about +10/month); recovery week near CTL - 30 TSS/day | Coach convention |
| TSB below about -20 at the end of a normal block (-40 only in a deliberate overload) | Next week is recovery | Coach convention |
| Garmin Load Ratio flags "high" | Note it; do not treat it as an injury risk signal on its own | Study (Impellizzeri) |
| Converting Garmin load to TSS | Do not; compute TSS from power, pace and HR files | Inference |

## Readiness signals change the next session, not the plan

### HRV gives a small edge, and only as a 7-day trend

HRV-guided training beats a fixed plan only by a small, non-significant margin. Endurance performance SMD was 0.20 (95% CI -0.09 to 0.48), although vagal HRV improved more ([Manresa-Rocamora 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jijerp/v18y2021i19p10299-d646878.html)). A second meta-analysis also found fewer non-responders with HRV guidance ([PubMed 34489178](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34489178/)). The working method is a 7-day rolling mean of morning lnRMSSD against a personal baseline. It needs **at least 3 valid readings per week** ([Plews 2014](https://www.pubmed.gov/24334285/)). A smallest-worthwhile-change band of about 0.5 x SD is used in practice ([TrainingPeaks](https://trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/hrv-guided-training); [Rouleur, Javaloyes](https://rouleur.cc/blogs/rouleur-performance/heart-rate-variability-all-you-need-to-know)). Direction alone misleads. In trained triathletes, lnRMSSD can **rise** during functional overreaching. A fall during a taper can mean freshness ([Plews thesis](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/7122)). In one case study of an elite triathlete, a falling 7-day mean and a falling day-to-day CV came before non-functional overreaching ([Plews 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22367011/)).

Garmin's HRV Status uses the same 7-day-vs-baseline logic and needs about three weeks to build a baseline ([Acibadem summary](https://acibademinternational.com/blog/garmin-body-battery-and-hrv-status-what-the-scores-actually-mean/)). But a 2025 validation found Garmin overnight HR and HRV agreed less well with the reference than Oura or Whoop did ([Physiological Reports 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12367097)). No peer-reviewed validation of Body Battery or Training Readiness was found. Training Readiness already blends sleep, recovery time, HRV status and acute load ([Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-31D23DBB-57C2-4DF7-A0C9-8D1A00AB4BE7/EN-US/GUID-C21BE0C8-A08E-4DA1-B6C6-2E0E2DDDB372.html)), so counting it with its inputs double-counts them. The sleep consensus says a fixed 7-9 h target is unlikely to suit everyone. **Habitual sleep under 7 h and fragmented sleep** are the markers of inadequate sleep ([Walsh 2021](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/16297/1/Walsh%202020_%20Sleep%20and%20the%20Athlete%202021_%20Consensus%20Review_%20BJSM_%20With%20Supplementary%20Material.pdf)).

| Signal | Adjustment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer than 3 valid HRV nights in the last 7 | No HRV signal; use RPE, sleep and resting HR only | Study |
| 7-day lnRMSSD inside baseline (30-60 days) mean ± 0.5 SD | Train as planned | Coach convention (SWC); method from studies |
| 7-day mean below mean - 0.5 SD for 2+ days AND one other marker agrees (resting HR up, poor sleep, high fatigue) | Replace the next intensity session with Z1-Z2 or rest; keep the week's long session if the athlete feels fine | Study for the method; thresholds are coach convention |
| 7-day mean above mean + 0.5 SD during a heavy block, with heavy fatigue or falling performance | Treat as possible overreaching, not freshness; insert recovery days | Study (Plews) plus inference |
| HRV falls during the taper | Do not react unless symptoms appear | Study (Plews thesis) |
| Garmin HRV Status "Low"/"Unbalanced" 1 day | Note only | Inference |
| Garmin HRV Status "Low" 3+ days plus one other marker | Lighten the next 2-3 days | Inference |
| Sleep under 7 h on 3+ of the last 5 nights | Cap the next day at endurance intensity; add no volume | Consensus (marker) plus inference (rule) |
| Waking resting HR about 5-7 bpm above the athlete's 30-day baseline | Easy day; ask about illness symptoms. No primary source for this threshold was found | Inference |
| Body Battery or Training Readiness low with normal HRV, resting HR and sleep | Ignore for decisions; weight raw HRV and resting HR trends above sleep, and sleep above composites | Inference (composites unvalidated) |

### Overreaching that lasts beyond two weeks is a medical and fueling question

The ECSS/ACSM consensus separates functional overreaching, which resolves in days to about 2 weeks and leads to a gain, from non-functional overreaching. NFOR means a stagnation or drop in performance "which will not resume for several weeks or months". Overtraining syndrome is diagnosed only after other causes are excluded ([Meeusen 2013](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Prevention_diagnosis_and_treatment_of_the_overtraining_syndrome_Joint_consensus_statement_of_the_European_College_of_Sport_Science_ECSS_and_the_American_College_of_Sports_Medicine_ACSM_/9627380/1/files/17276126.pdf)). The IOC 2023 REDs statement stresses the overlap in symptoms between REDs and overtraining. It says to rule out low energy availability and low carbohydrate availability first, and it lists low libido and fewer morning erections as emerging signs in men ([IOC REDs 2023](https://forening.sls.se/media/a4cl4crj/idrottsnutrition_2023-ioc-consenses-on-red-s-1.pdf)).

| Signal | Adjustment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Performance down and fatigue high, recovering within 1-2 weeks of reduced load | Functional overreaching; resume the plan | Consensus |
| Cannot hit usual power/pace for over 2 weeks despite reduced load | Easy volume only; tell the athlete to see a doctor; screen for REDs (intake vs training, unplanned weight loss, menstrual changes, low libido, frequent illness, bone pain) | Consensus plus inference |

### Illness follows the IOC severity grades, not the neck check

The IOC 2022 consensus states that the "neck check" has no scientific basis. It grades respiratory illness by severity instead. **Mild** means upper-respiratory symptoms only, no systemic symptoms, resolving in under 3 days. **Moderate** means lower-respiratory symptoms such as cough or breathlessness. **Severe** means systemic symptoms such as fever over 38 °C, chills, aches, chest pain, palpitations or resting tachycardia. Over 80% of athlete infections are mild, mean symptom duration is 7 days, and return takes 0 to about 8 days. Before moderate or hard training returns, the athlete does an **exercise challenge test**: 10-20 minutes at 60-70% of normal intensity. The athlete stops and sees a doctor if fatigue, breathlessness, chest pain, dizziness, palpitations, unusually high HR or "not feeling well" appear during the test or within 24 h. The consensus also gives the strongest single argument for caution. Athletes completing **over 80% of planned training weeks were 7x more likely to reach their goal**, and each week with modified training cut the chance by 26% ([IOC 2022](https://www.casem-acmse.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/1066.full_.pdf)).

| Signal | Adjustment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Fever over 38 °C, chills, body aches, chest pain, palpitations, GI illness, or raised resting HR with symptoms | No training; rest; doctor if severe or over 7 days | Consensus |
| Cough or breathlessness (moderate) | Stop training until resolved; doctor-guided return | Consensus |
| Mild upper-only symptoms | Easy Z1-Z2 only; no intensity, no long sessions, until resolved | Consensus plus inference |
| Symptom-free 24-48 h | Exercise challenge test (10-20 min at 60-70%) | Consensus (test); coach convention (24-48 h) |
| Challenge test normal and no symptoms within 24 h | 2-3 days easy at reduced duration; add intensity only when HRV and resting HR are near baseline | Inference consistent with IOC |
| Challenge test abnormal | Stop; refer to a doctor | Consensus |

### Missed sessions are skipped, not stacked

Coaches agree on two points: do not make up missed workouts, and continue the plan from the current day ([Tri247](https://www.tri247.com/guides/stay-on-track-after-missing-workout)). TrainerRoad says a few missed days need no change. More than a week missed means a progression step was lost ([TrainerRoad via Zeno](https://zenocycleparts.com/blogs/trainerroad-blog/how-do-i-adjust-my-training-plan-following-time-off)). Detraining is real but slow at first. Highly trained athletes lose 4-14% of VO2max within 4 weeks, driven by a 5-12% fall in blood volume ([Mujika & Padilla review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9398774/)). Classic data show -7% at 21 days and -16% at 56 days of inactivity ([TrainingPeaks, Coyle 1984](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/what-happens-to-your-body-during-a-training-break/)). A 2024 review notes a "dearth of data" in this area ([Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1334766/full)). No source ranks session priority, so the ranking below is inference.

| Signal | Adjustment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 day missed | Skip it. If it was the key session, move it to the next easy day only if no hard session falls within 24 h either side | Coach convention plus inference |
| 2-4 days missed | Resume at today; drop one intensity session in the return week | Inference |
| 5-10 days missed (or illness) | 3-5 days at 50-70% volume, Z1-Z2 only, then rejoin; do not repeat lost weeks unless over 8 weeks remain | Coach convention plus inference |
| 2+ weeks missed | Repeat the last completed build week; rebuild over about half to all of the time missed. Under 6 weeks to race: lower the goal, do not compress load | Study (detraining) plus inference |
| Only some sessions fit this week | Keep, in order: long ride, long run, brick/race-specific, one quality bike, swim (2/wk for feel), strength, recovery sessions | Inference |
| High work stress, travel, poor sleep | Cut volume and intensity rather than moving sessions; count life stress as load | Coach convention plus inference |

### Running carries most of the injury risk, so cap single runs

Running caused **45-92% of injuries** in short-course triathlon studies ([Guevara 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10980869)). In long-distance triathlon, the knee and spine are the most common injury sites ([Rhind 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36898525/)). The strongest load rule comes from Garmin-RUNSAFE: 5,205 runners and about 588,000 sessions over 18 months. A single run 10-30% longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days gave a hazard ratio of **1.64**; 30-100% longer gave 1.52; over 100% longer gave 2.28. Weekly distance change and ACWR showed no clear link. Even 1-10% increases carried some risk ([Frandsen 2025, BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751)). For novices, an older cohort suggests weekly run distance should grow less than 30% over 2 weeks ([Nielsen 2014](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn)). The data cover running only, not cycling or swimming.

| Signal | Adjustment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Planned run longer than 1.10 x the longest run in the last 30 days | Cut it to 1.10x or less; target +5-10% steps for the long run | Study (large cohort) |
| Runs were missed (illness, travel) | The cap falls with the 30-day max; recompute it before each long run | Study plus inference |
| Novice runner | Weekly run distance up less than 30% over 2 weeks; extra aerobic volume goes on the bike | Study plus inference |
| Pain that changes gait, worsens during the run or persists next morning | Stop running; replace with bike or aqua-jogging at the same duration and intensity; return when walking and hopping are pain-free | Inference |
| Local bone pain (shin, foot, hip) | No running; see a doctor; screen for REDs | Inference consistent with consensus |
| Training run TSS near race-day run TSS (190-250) | Do not plan it | Coach convention |

## Heavy lifting twice a week helps endurance if it sits on hard days

### Concurrent training costs little strength and adds economy

The interference effect is small for this athlete. A 43-study meta-analysis found that adding endurance work does not reduce gains in maximal strength (SMD -0.06) or muscle size (SMD -0.01). It does reduce explosive strength gains (SMD -0.28), but only when both are in the same session. With at least 3 h between sessions, the effect was gone. Age (under or over 40) did not moderate the result ([Schumann 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34878640/)). In trained lifters, same-session pairing reduced lower-body 1RM gains (ES -0.66) ([Petré 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)). Lifting before endurance in a combined session gave about 7% more lower-body strength gain, with no effect on VO2max ([Eddens 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28917030/)).

The payoff is economy, not VO2max. In runners, **high-load (at least 80% 1RM) and combined heavy-plus-plyometric programs improved running economy**. Submaximal-load (40-79% 1RM) and isometric training did not ([Llanos-Lagos 2024, RE](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37272/)). In 38 studies, high-load training had a moderate effect on time-trial performance (ES -0.469) and combined methods a large one (ES -1.035). No method changed VO2max ([Llanos-Lagos 2024, performance](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/37960/1/s40279-024-02018-z.pdf)). In 262 trained cyclists, heavy strength training improved cycling efficiency (ES 0.353) and time-trial performance (ES 0.463), at low certainty ([Llanos-Lagos 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-025-05883-2)). For maintenance, well-trained cyclists lifted 2x/week for 12 weeks, then **1x/week for 13 weeks kept the strength and muscle gains** ([Rønnestad 2010](https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/170841/Raastad%20EurJApplPhys%202010.pdf?sequence=1)). In soccer players, one session every 2 weeks was not enough ([Rønnestad 2011](https://www.scribd.com/document/551184767/Rønnestad-2011)). Strength training also has meta-analytic support for reducing sports injuries ([Lauersen 2014](https://hvlopen.brage.unit.no/hvlopen-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/2585394/lauersen.pdf?sequence=4)). Swimming and cycling load bone poorly, and masters women lose bone density despite high training volume ([PubMed 11584034](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11584034/)). No strength trial exists in triathletes, and no trial uses Ironman race outcomes.

### Placement matters more than the program

A lower-body session impaired running economy at 24 and 48 h ([Eur J Appl Physiol 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-024-05422-5)). Muscle damage worsens high-intensity running at 24 and 48 h, and low-intensity running at 48 h ([Burt et al., Keele](https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/output/1323629/the-effects-of-exercise-induced-muscle-damage-on-varying-intensities-of-endurance-running-performance-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)). DOMS is lower after the second bout of a new exercise ([PMC6548784](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6548784)), so new exercises belong in the off-season.

| Phase | Strength prescription | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Off-season / general prep (8-12 wk) | 2-3/wk. Weeks 1-3: 2-3 x 8-12 at RIR 3-4. Then 3-4 x 3-6 at about 80-90% 1RM, RIR 1-3. Add plyometrics (2-4 x 5-10 contacts) | Study for load and frequency; set/rep scheme is coach convention |
| Base / early build | 2/wk, 2-4 x 3-6, RIR 2-3, 2-4 lower-body exercises | Study plus coach convention |
| Last 6-10 weeks | 1-2/wk, 2-3 x 3-5, load kept high (RIR 2-3); no new exercises, no eccentric-heavy work | Coach convention |
| Competition / peak | 1/wk minimum to maintain; never less than weekly | Study |
| Taper | Last heavy session about 7-10 days out, then light activation only | Coach convention (untested) |
| Post-race | 1-2 weeks off heavy lifting, then restart at anatomical adaptation | Inference |
| Athlete 40+ (esp. peri/post-menopausal women) | Keep 1-2 heavy sessions/wk all year plus some impact work | Study (bone) plus inference |
| Exercise menu | Squat or trap-bar deadlift, RDL, split squat, step-up, single-leg RDL, heavy calf raises (straight and bent knee), hip thrust, anti-rotation trunk, pull-ups/rows plus shoulder external rotation | Coach consensus; trials used mostly squat, leg press, deadlift, step-up, calf raise |

| Signal (from LogEveryLift and Garmin) | Adjustment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Key endurance session in the same day | Lift after it, 3-6+ h later (AM key session, PM lift); "hard days hard" | Study (3 h cut-off); 6 h is coach convention |
| Same session unavoidable | Lift first, unless the endurance session is the day's priority | Study |
| Long run, long ride, brick or interval run within 48 h | No heavy lower-body lifting; upper-body/pull is fine | Study (RE impairment 24-48 h) plus inference |
| Build/peak conflict between a lift and a key session | The key endurance session wins; move or shrink the lift | Coach convention |
| Lower-limb DOMS still present after 48 h, or on a key-session day | Cut lifting volume 30-50% (fewer sets), keep load, raise RIR to 3-4 | Study (EIMD) plus inference |
| Key session missed or clearly below target the day after a lift | Same volume cut; move the lift further from that session type | Inference |
| Loads at the same RIR fall more than 5-10% across two sessions | Treat as systemic fatigue; check HRV, sleep and fuel; cut volume | Inference |
| Logged RIR well above target at the prescribed load | Raise the load | Inference |
| Volume-type "toning" sets (12-20 reps, light) as the main stimulus | Replace them with heavy 3-6 rep work; they do not improve economy | Study |

## Fueling is trained like a fourth discipline

### 60-90 g/h is the target, and more needs a trained gut

The consensus ceiling is **up to 90 g/h of glucose plus fructose for exercise over about 2.5 h** ([Jeukendrup 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24791914/); [mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/2015/05/27/recommendations-for-carb-intake-during-exercise)). At 120 g/h, a 1:0.8 maltodextrin:fructose mix raises exogenous oxidation above a 90 g/h 2:1 mix ([Podlogar 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9560939)). Drink, gel and chew forms work equally ([Hearris 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35446596/)). But a 2025 review found **no clear evidence that over 90 g/h improves performance**. In one study, TT power was highest at 90 g/h ([Wilson 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02372-6)). In real races, athletes averaged **62-71 g/h** at Ironman Hawaii and Germany. Higher intake went with faster finishes and also with more nausea and flatulence. About 31% had serious GI symptoms ([Pfeiffer 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21775906/)). That link is observational: fitter athletes may both eat more and race faster. Power at LT1 explained 36% of the variation in exogenous oxidation ([Wilson 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02372-6)), so slower age-groupers may have a lower useful ceiling.

Gut training works on symptoms. Two weeks of 90 g/h during daily runs cut GI symptoms and malabsorption, but feeding tolerance in the challenge test did not change ([Miall 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28508559/)). A systematic review found about a 47% drop in gut discomfort ([Sports Med 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-023-01841-0.pdf)). Practitioners use 5-10 weeks with 2-3 sessions a week, and advise racing at about 80% of the tolerance reached in training ([Precision Hydration](https://www.precisionhydration.com/performance-advice/nutrition/can-you-train-your-gut/)). In women at the 2024 World Championships, pre-race fibre correlated with GI symptoms, and pre-race carbohydrate correlated with faster finishes (r = -0.50) ([Fortis 2026](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/28491/)).

### Drink to a measured sweat rate and never gain weight

Sweat sodium varies about 10-fold, roughly **230-2,070 mg/L** ([GSSI](https://performancepartner.gatorade.com/content/resources/pdfs/understanding-athletes-sweat.pdf)). Predicted whole-body sweat sodium averaged 35.9 mmol/L (about 825 mg/L) in 506 athletes ([Baker 2016](https://www.gssiweb.org/en/research/Article/normative-data-for-regional-sweat-sodium-concentration-and-whole-body-sweating-rate-in-athletes-)). Drinking too much is the primary cause of exercise-associated hyponatremia ([Hew-Butler 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4470181)). At Ironman New Zealand, 8 of 11 severe cases kept or gained body mass during the race ([Speedy, CMAJ](https://cmaj.ca/content/163/4/439)). Median intake in two recent Ironman-distance races was **800-900 ml/h**, and most athletes stayed eunatremic ([Frontiers in Nutrition](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2021.781229/pdf)). These are observed medians, not a recommendation.

### Daily intake follows the day's load

The ACSM/AND/DC bands scale carbohydrate from **3-5 g/kg/day** on light days to **8-12 g/kg/day** on very big days, with protein 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day ([ACSM/AND/DC 2016](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/880yy/position-of-the-academy-of-nutrition-and-dietetics-dietitians-of-canada-and-the-american-college-of-sports-medicine-nutrition-and-athletic-performance)). Tracer data put the protein need of trained endurance athletes at about 1.55-1.75 g/kg/day ([Kato 2016 summary](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol232/kato.htm)). The IOC 2023 statement no longer uses 30 kcal/kg FFM/day as a diagnostic cut-off. It notes that men may tolerate 9-25 before problems ([IOC REDs 2023](https://forening.sls.se/media/a4cl4crj/idrottsnutrition_2023-ioc-consenses-on-red-s-1.pdf)). Carbohydrate periodisation ("train low") does not improve performance on average (SMD 0.17, p = 0.29) ([Gejl & Nybo 2021](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-021-00435-3)), though one triathlete sleep-low study found a 2.9% faster 10 km ([Marquet 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/2605/1/Marquet%20et%20al%20%20%28MSSE%29%202016%20Enhanced%20endurance%20performance%20by%20periodization%20of%20CHO%20intake-SL%20strategy%20-.pdf)). Trained athletes can supercompensate glycogen in 24-48 h without depletion ([Burke 2011](https://typeset.io/pdf/carbohydrates-for-training-and-competition-ei43zrhkcx.pdf)). Caffeine at **3-6 mg/kg** is the one well-supported ergogenic aid. 9 mg/kg adds side effects with no extra benefit ([Guest 2021, ISSN](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7777221)). Any nitrate benefit is small and depends on context ([Xie 2026](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Xie-2026-Nitrates-1.pdf)).

| If | Then | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Race fueling target, untrained gut | Bike 60-80 g/h, run 50-70 g/h; 2:1 glucose:fructose | Consensus plus study; bike/run split is inference |
| Gut trained to 90 g/h with no symptoms | Bike up to 90 g/h; above 90-100 g/h use about 1:0.8; 90-120 g/h only after 6-10+ weeks without symptoms | Study (oxidation) plus inference |
| Gut training block | 2-3 long sessions/wk with race products; +10 g/h every 1-2 weeks; allow 8-12 weeks from 60 to 90 g/h; include runs off the bike | Coach convention plus inference |
| GI score 4/10 or more after a fueled session | Hold the rate or step back one level | Inference |
| Race-day plan | Race at about 80% of the highest tolerance proved in training | Coach convention |
| Session over 2 h, long brick, long run over 90 min, race simulation | Practise full race fueling with race products | Coach convention |
| Easy session under 90 min | Water only is fine | Coach convention |
| Sweat-rate test | Nude weight before and after 60 min at race effort in race-like heat: (pre - post + fluid - urine) / hours; repeat in 2-3 temperatures | Inference from consensus method |
| Setting fluid | Replace about 50-80% of sweat loss, usually 400-900 ml/h; never above sweat rate; 1-3% body-mass loss by the finish is normal | Consensus plus inference |
| Body mass up after a long session | Red flag for over-drinking; cut planned fluid | Study |
| Sodium | About 300-600 mg/h default (500-1,000 mg per litre drunk); more for salty sweaters in heat; volume, not sodium, prevents hyponatremia | Consensus (variation) plus inference (doses) |
| Daily carbohydrate | Rest/easy 3-5 g/kg; 1-2 h day 5-7; 2-4 h day 7-10; long brick or big weekend day 8-12 | Consensus |
| Daily protein (athlete who lifts) | 1.6-2.0 g/kg/day in 4-5 feeds of 20-40 g, one after lifting | Study plus inference |
| Train-low | At most 1-3 easy low-glycogen sessions/wk; never on key days, gut-training days, or with weight-loss goals | Study (no average benefit) plus inference |
| Two sessions under 8 h apart | 1.0-1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate for the first 4 h | Consensus |
| 2 days before race | 8-12 g/kg/day, low fibre, low fat; warn that a 1-2 kg weight gain is normal | Consensus plus inference |
| Race morning | 1-3 g/kg carbohydrate 2-3 h before the start, familiar and low fibre | Consensus |
| Caffeine | About 3 mg/kg pre-start or early bike, plus 1-3 mg/kg in the late bike and run; 3-6 mg/kg total; test in training | Consensus plus inference |
| Unplanned weight loss over 2-3 weeks, falling performance at stable load, low libido, menstrual change, frequent illness | Raise carbohydrate and total energy first; refer to a doctor or dietitian | Consensus plus inference |

Estimating carbohydrate use from data is rough. Energy cost is about bike work (kJ) divided by gross efficiency of about 0.24 ([Roadman Cycling](https://roadmancycling.com/tools/calories)). Users and coaches note that app "carbs used" values link only loosely to true oxidation ([intervals.icu forum](https://forum.intervals.icu/t/please-validate-the-cho-used-algorithm/110935.md)). The coach should use such estimates to explain why fueling matters, not to set exact intake.

## Plans become data, and every change becomes a proposal the athlete accepts

### Every workout format reduces to the same tree

intervals.icu text, Garmin's workout JSON, FIT `workout_step` messages and Zwift `.zwo` all share one shape. A workout holds ordered steps. Each step has an intent, an end condition (time, distance or lap button), and an optional target (power, HR, pace or cadence) as a zone or a low-high range. A repeat node wraps child steps with a count ([intervals.icu builder](https://forum.intervals.icu/t/workout-builder/1163/1.md); [garmin-connect-mcp](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/etweisberg/garmin-connect-mcp/tools/create-workout); [tormoder/fit](https://pkg.go.dev/github.com/tormoder/fit)). intervals.icu accepts `80%` of FTP, `100% LTHR`, zones such as `Z2 HR`, ranges and ramps. It does not support nested repeats ([intervals.icu builder](https://forum.intervals.icu/t/workout-builder/1163/1.md)). FIT tells absolute values from relative ones with an offset (bpm + 100, watts + 1000). When the offset was missing, a Garmin device read 200-250 W as 200-250% FTP ([kaiord PR #1316](https://github.com/pablo-albaladejo/kaiord/pull/1316)). The lesson for the coach is to store targets relative to an anchor and resolve them to watts, bpm or pace only at export. Then a retest updates every future workout at once.

```text
Plan{goal, race_date, hours_budget, block_pattern(2:1|3:1)}
 └ Phase{type: base|build|peak|taper|recovery, start, end}
    └ Week{index, focus, planned_load, is_recovery}
       └ PlannedSession{date, sport: swim|bike|run|brick|strength, priority: key|support|optional,
                        workout_id | strength_template_id, planned_duration, planned_distance, planned_load}
Workout{sport, name, steps[]}
 Step = Executable{intent: warmup|interval|recovery|cooldown|rest,
                   end: {type: time|distance|lap, value},
                   target?: {metric: power|hr|pace|speed|cadence, anchor: ftp|lthr|threshold_pace|css|absolute,
                             low, high, zone?}, cue?}
      | Repeat{count, children[]}            (one level of nesting; flatten on export)
Anchor{sport, kind: ftp|lthr|threshold_pace|css|vt1, value, measured_on, source: test|estimate}
CompletedSession{activity_id, planned_session_id?, actual_load, compliance_ratio, compliance_band, rpe?, gi_score?}
```

This model is inference from the formats above. Store pace internally as speed (m/s), because pace ranges invert and cause bugs. Strength is one more `PlannedSession` sport with a load estimate (session RPE x minutes), so it counts against the week. TrainingPeaks' compliance colours are the de facto standard. **Green** means within ±20% of plan. **Yellow** means 50-79% or 121-150%. **Orange** means more than 50% off. **Red** means missed. TSS is the default metric, then duration, then distance ([TrainingPeaks help](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204861204)).

### Getting workouts onto a Garmin is the hard part in late 2026

The only sanctioned push route is Garmin's Training API. It is business-tier only and publishes to the user's Garmin Connect calendar after consent ([Garmin Training API](https://developer.garmin.com/gc-developer-program/training-api)). New applications were paused from about spring 2026. Blocks was told in September that no new connections were accepted, and it routes Garmin users through intervals.icu or FIT files ([the5krunner, Sep 2026](https://the5krunner.com/2026/09/14/garmin-developer-api-access-paused/)). The October 2026 personal API key is **pull-only and for personal use**. It cannot send a session to the watch ([the5krunner, Oct 2026](https://the5krunner.com/2026/10/22/garmin-personal-api/)). The project's own `BACKLOG.md` quotes the supplemental terms: a key "may be used only by you". So the practical routes are an intervals.icu bridge (the athlete's account already holds Garmin push rights), a FIT download the athlete side-loads, or a Training API application. The unofficial workout-service suits only the owner's own account.

### Adaptive products change which session runs, not what is inside it

TrainerRoad keeps a 1-10 Progression Level per zone. A five-point post-workout survey (Easy to All Out) feeds it. The app shows "Adaptations Pending", and the user taps **"Accept Adaptations"**. Adaptations change which workout is recommended, not the workout's structure. Adaptive Training can be switched off ([TrainerRoad](https://www.trainerroad.com/blog/how-to-use-adaptive-training/); [smartbiketrainers](https://www.smartbiketrainers.com/trainerroad-adaptive-training-13349)). Garmin's adaptive run coach rebuilds the schedule daily from Training Readiness, but users report minimal changes and repetition after about 8 weeks ([athletedata.health](https://www.athletedata.health/guides/garmin-coach-vs-ai-coach); [shoulditrain](https://www.shoulditrain.com/blog/garmin-coach-review)). TriDot is criticised for minimal adaptation ([Transition](https://www.transition.fun/blog/transition-vs-tridot-athletica-humango-ai-endurance-trainingpeaks/)). Athletica swaps sessions for ones of similar effort ([Athletica forum](https://forum.athletica.ai/t/athletica-vs-humango-vs-tridot/1930)). Community MCP servers expose read tools (activities, sleep, HRV, resting HR), write tools (create, update or delete planned workouts) and format helpers. Several broker writes through intervals.icu so no Garmin credentials are shared ([garmin-claude-mcp](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/SaraR99/garmin-claude-mcp)). One server ships the workout syntax as an MCP resource so the model writes valid text ([domestique](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@gesteves/domestique/blob/20812aa55701874b27ac02c5fde692861f748452/src/resources/cycling-workout-syntax.md)). Few servers enforce progression limits. Long-term memory and approval before writes are mostly left to the client.

| Design rule for the AI coach | Reason | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The model writes proposals, not the plan: `propose_plan_changes(changes[], rationale)`; the app applies them only after the athlete accepts | Matches TrainerRoad's accept pattern; keeps the athlete in control | Coach/product convention plus inference |
| Show each change as a diff with the triggering signal ("HRV 7-day mean below band 3 days, sleep 6.1 h: Thursday threshold run becomes 50 min Z2") | Trust comes from visible reasons; templated, unexplained changes draw the main criticism | Inference from product reviews |
| Enforce guardrails in code, not in the prompt: CTL ramp cap, 1.10x long-run cap, max key sessions per week, no hard session after a flagged low-readiness day, no heavy legs within 48 h of a key run, schema validation of every workout | Prompts drift; few MCP servers enforce limits | Inference |
| Adapt at the session level (swap, move, scale duration) before editing interval internals | What working products do; easier to explain and reverse | Coach/product convention |
| Keep a dated athlete profile as long-term memory: anchors, race, hours budget, injury history, run durability, GI tolerance, sweat rate, preferences | Every rule above needs these inputs | Inference |
| Ask for cheap inputs: session RPE, a GI score after fueled sessions, annotated illness/travel days | High-value signals that the watch cannot give | Coach/product convention plus inference |
| Let the athlete switch adaptation off or overrule it, and log the override | TrainerRoad offers an off switch; overrides are data | Product convention plus inference |
| Never act on one bad day; require two agreeing signals for any downgrade, and never upgrade from a single good day | HRV meta-analyses show small effects; composite scores are unvalidated | Study plus inference |

## Conclusion

The evidence is strongest where an AI coach is most tempted to be clever, and it mostly says do less. HRV guidance, polarized versus pyramidal splits, readiness scores and 120 g/h fueling all show small, unproven or vendor-only benefits. The strongest signals are blunt: completion rate predicts the goal, one oversized run predicts injury, and heavy lifting at the right load and placement improves economy. The coach is better framed as a constraint system than as an optimiser. It holds a plan of 3-week blocks, refuses changes that break the ramp, long-run, placement and illness rules, and spends its intelligence explaining trade-offs to the athlete. Much of what matters still lacks direct evidence: Ironman-specific taper trials, triathlete strength trials, tested recovery-week cuts, a validated resting-HR illness threshold, and race-day TSB targets. The coach should say so when it relies on those rules.

The LogEveryLift position is unusual and worth using. Commercial triathlon engines treat strength as an afterthought. This coach can see RIR, load trends and lifting placement next to Garmin HRV and session files. So it can enforce the one interaction the research quantifies well, the 24-48 h economy cost of heavy legs, and use falling strength at the same RIR as a fatigue signal. The binding constraint is not physiology but distribution. Until Garmin's Training API opens to the app, workouts reach the watch through intervals.icu or FIT files, and the personal key's terms limit any Garmin reading to the owner's own account.
